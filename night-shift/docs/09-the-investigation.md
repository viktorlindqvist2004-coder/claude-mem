# The Investigation — Who Is the Night Manager?

The win condition of Night Shift is not surviving. It is **naming him**, and making the
case stick with a detective who does not believe you.

---

## 1. The question

Across five nights the crew gathers evidence from a 1998 missing-persons case that was
never closed. On the last night they present it to Northmoor Police and answer one
question:

> **Who is the Night Manager?**

Answer it correctly, with evidence, and the mall closes. Answer it wrongly and he keeps the
building — and keeps whoever you accused.

The answer is **Elias Wren**. Nothing in the game ever says so. Six other options on the
board look better than he does for most of the playthrough, and one of them is the police's
own theory.

---

## 2. Fairness contract

A mystery is only hard in a good way if it is honest. These are rules, not aims.

1. **Every elimination is findable.** Each wrong suspect has exactly one piece of hard
   evidence that rules them out, and that evidence is obtainable by a crew that searches.
2. **No clue is permanently missable.** If a night is finished without a clue, it can be
   recovered on a later night in a secondary location. Clue locations are seeded from a
   pool of three per clue; the clue set is not.
3. **No NPC ever states the conclusion.** Calder reasons, argues, corroborates and is
   wrong. She never solves it.
4. **The game never draws a line for the player.** Clues are logged with what they *are*
   and where they were found. Tagging them to a suspect is entirely the crew's job.
5. **Six different kinds of evidence eliminate the six wrong suspects** — a dated invoice,
   an audio comparison, a postcard, an employment record, a handwriting match, and a living
   witness. You cannot solve this by exhausting one mechanic.
6. **Guessing is a bad strategy and the game says so.** Calder will not back an accusation
   that lacks three valid supporting clues. She can be overridden, at the cost of a full
   replay of Night 5 and a harder one.

**Calibration target:** under 15 % of first-time crews should name him correctly on the
first attempt. Around 60 % should get there by their second. A crew that has found every
clue and talked to each other should feel that it was obvious in hindsight — and a crew
that has not should feel that it was gettable.

---

## 3. The suspect board

Seven options. All but one have a genuine, supported case against them.

### 1 — ELIAS WREN · Assistant Stationmaster, Northmoor Station, 1930–1971 · **the answer**
*Why he looks wrong:* he never worked at the mall, never set foot in it, and died fifteen
years before it opened. Every instinct the player has says the culprit must be connected to
the building. He is connected to the **clock**, which is a different thing, and that gap is
the whole difficulty of the mystery.

*What convicts him:* the auction receipt linking the clock to Northmoor Station; the
dedication plate behind the dial; the eleven-minute intervals in the PA logbook matching the
eleven minutes it took to wind that clock by hand; and every railway announcement he has
been making at the crew for five nights without any of them noticing it was a railway
announcement.

---

### 2 — ERIC HALVARD · Developer, built the Galleria, died 1994
*The case against:* he bought the clock. It is his mall. He signed off the 1989 complaint
and had a lock fitted on the PA room — a lock later found fitted from the inside. A man who
wanted his mall open forever is the tidiest villain available, and players reach for him
first.

*What clears him:* the **opening-day cassette** (Night 1). Halvard's recorded voice is
nothing like the PA voice — different cadence, different register, and he mispronounces
"galleria". And the voice did not change in 1994 when he died, which the PA logbook shows.
**Eliminated by audio comparison.**

---

### 3 — WALT · Night security, 1994–1998 · **the police's theory**
*The case against:* he cut the power. His own tape is a statement of intent. He is the only
one of the four whose name tag is *handed to you* rather than found. "Tell them to stop
opening" reads exactly like a confession. And in 1998 Northmoor Police concluded, on the
evidence they had, that Walt did something to the other three and left.

**Calder believes this.** She will say so on every debrief, she will argue it well, and she
is the authority figure in the game. This is the single strongest red herring in the design
precisely because it comes with a badge.

*What clears him:* the **personnel record** (Night 3) puts his start date at 1994, and the
**lock invoice** (Night 3) dates the first PA complaint to April 1989. The voice predates
him by five years. **Eliminated by employment dates.**

---

### 4 — IVOR KASK · "Night Manager", on the payroll for six weeks in 1989
*The case against:* the payroll ledger shows a night manager post created in spring 1989,
filled by one I. Kask, then deleted and never refilled. No address. No tax number. Paid in
cash. In the 1989 staff photograph one figure's face is out of focus, and it is out of focus
in every copy. The dates line up with the complaints perfectly.

*What clears him:* a **postcard in the lost property drawer** (Night 3), Gothenburg, 1992,
signed *Ivor*. He got out. And the reversal is the best moment on the board — the dates line
up because **Kask is the one who filed the complaints.** The man who looks most like the
Night Manager is the first witness, and his complaint form is a key piece of the case
against Wren. **Eliminated by a physical object that is very easy to walk past.**

---

### 5 — MARGARET VALE · Centre Manager, 1986–1998 · **guilty of something else**
*The case against:* she signed the lock installation. She buried the complaints. She
redated the 1998 incident report. She kept the night rota running after four people
vanished off it. And in 1987 she signed the directory amendment that deleted Floor 3.

*What clears her:* she is **alive**, in a care home in Northmoor, and if the crew get her
name to Calder by Night 4 the detective obtains a statement. It is the best clue delivery in
the game — an eighty-one-year-old woman, entirely lucid:

> "We knew. Of course we knew. You don't work somewhere fourteen years and not know.
> We just kept it open, because that's what you do with a shopping centre. I took the third
> floor off the directory in eighty-seven because I'd *been* on it, and I wasn't having
> customers up there.
> No, I never met him. He isn't a him you meet. He's a him you get a memo from."

She covered it up for eleven years. She did not cause it. **Eliminated by a living
witness.**

---

### 6 — NORA · Checkout, 1996–1998
*The case against:* your guide, and the most suspicious person in the game if you look at
her sideways. Her rules sometimes send you wrong. She knew far more than a cashier should.
She prepared for something. The forged lines on the sheet are signed **"— N."** And the
Night Manager occasionally *defends* her, which reads as protecting an accomplice.

*What clears her:* the **handwriting**. The forged lines are neat, evenly spaced and never
cross anything out; her list is slanted ballpoint that skips, with two rules struck through
by her own hand. The game teaches this tell on Night 2 for other reasons and then asks the
crew to use it on the person they trust most. **Eliminated by handwriting.**

---

### 7 — "NOBODY. THE BUILDING ITSELF."
The clever answer. It is thematically close, it is what the crew will have been saying to
each other since Night 2, and it is wrong.

Calder's reply, and the thesis of the game in one line:

> "A building doesn't keep a rota. Somebody's been signing these off every week since
> eighty-six. Find me the somebody."

### The voice log

The one clue the crew earns by listening rather than searching. Whenever the Night Manager
uses a railway announcement phrase, it is logged verbatim with a timestamp. He does this
constantly, in a shopping centre, and nobody notices for four nights.

> "Attention, please."
> "Mind the step."
> "The service to the south."
> "There will be no further departures."
> "Stand away from the doors."
> "Thank you for your patience. We are running a little behind."

Paired with C18 (the clock came from a station) and C19 (eleven minutes, every night, for
forty years), this is the spine of the case. It is also the reason a crew that solves it
feels clever rather than lucky: they solved it by paying attention to a character they never
saw.

## 5. DS Ruth Calder

Northmoor Police. Fifty. In 1998 she was a twenty-two-year-old probationer on the original
callout, and she has never let the file go. She is competent, tired, dry, and **wrong**.

She contacts the crew after Night 1 — the night-staffing contract on the Galleria changed
hands, she has an alert on it, and for the first time in twenty-eight years somebody went in
and came out.

**Her function:**
- **Between nights**, a debrief at the station: an interview room, a corkboard, and a
  detective asking what you found and what you think it means. This replaces the shop menu
  as the between-nights beat.
- **She corroborates.** Give her a name and she runs it: employment records, death
  certificates, the 1971 station file, Margaret Vale's statement. This is how external
  evidence enters a game set inside one building.
- **She argues.** Her theory is Walt, and she defends it well, because on the evidence
  available to a police officer it is the correct conclusion.
- **She warns you off the answer.** When Wren's name first comes up:

  > "A man who died in nineteen seventy-one did not file a complaint in eighty-nine.
  > I know what this place is doing to you. Don't bring me ghosts. Bring me a person."

  A good detective applying good reasoning to a case where the rules do not hold. Convincing
  her is the actual final boss of the game.

**Hint ladder** for stalled crews, delivered as her own thinking so it never breaks
character: after two failed attempts she starts re-reading the file aloud, and each pass
foregrounds one unexamined clue. She never names a suspect she has not been given.

## 6. The accusation

### Sequence, Night 5

1. **05:00** — the crew radios Calder from the security office. The case board opens: seven
   suspects, evidence slots, everything the crew has logged.
2. **Choose one suspect and three supporting clues.** In a full crew this is a nomination
   and a majority vote; the holder of the case file breaks ties. The argument this produces
   is the best four minutes in the game and it should not be rushed — the clock is paused
   here, deliberately.
3. **Calder tests it.** If the three clues do not support the name, she pushes back once and
   returns the board. One free correction. She is not trying to trick you.
4. **She gives you the name over the radio**, along with whatever the 1971 station file gave
   her.
5. **05:59, at the Great Clock.** One player winds the hands forward from 3:33 to 6:00 —
   forty seconds, locked in place — while the rest hold him off with focus beams, and one of
   them says the name into the PA.

### Outcomes

| Accusation | Result |
| --- | --- |
| **Wren, three valid clues** | **Closing Time.** The clock strikes six. The four walk out. |
| **Wren, forced through without support** | The clock winds, but he was never properly named. The mall closes; the PA light outside an empty office on a floor that does not exist stays lit. |
| **Halvard, Kask, Vale or "the building"** | He takes the accusation as a resignation letter and does not accept it. Soft fail: every clock snaps to 3:33, the muzak restarts, Night 5 begins again, harder — he now knows you are guessing. |
| **Walt or Nora** | The worst ending in the game. You have accused a missing person of their own disappearance, in a building that files paperwork. Their shade does not leave with the others. Night 5 restarts and there are three at the entrance for the rest of that save, unless the crew reaches the true ending later and gets them back. |

**Evidence is never lost on a restart.** A wrong accusation costs a replay and a harder
night, not an hour of collecting. The mystery converges; it does not reset.

**Anti-brute-force:** there are not three valid supporting clues for any wrong suspect, so
working through the board one name at a time means overriding a detective who is telling you
the case does not hold, seven times, at twenty minutes a go, while the game gets harder each
time. The friction is honest and it is enough.

## 7. The true ending — "Elias Wren"

Naming him correctly wins. Naming him correctly **with all five tapes and all four name
tags** does something else.

Tape 5 is not a clue. It is inside the clock movement, it is a 1971 station reel, and it is
his own voice — the last announcement at Northmoor Station, and then thirty seconds where he
forgets the microphone is open.

With the tape in hand, the crew can put it through the PA instead of speaking the name. He
hears himself, in 1971, saying:

> "Forty-one years. And not one of them thought to ask what I'm meant to do at six o'clock
> in the morning."

And then, close to the microphone, almost cheerful:

> "Well. Somebody's got to keep it open."

He does not fight that. There is nothing to hold off for forty seconds. The clock strikes
six on its own.

There are five people at the entrance in the morning. He is at the back, in a
stationmaster's coat, waiting for the others to go first, because that is what you do at the
end of a shift.

## 8. What this changed elsewhere

- **Tape 4 no longer names him.** Malik still works out the *mechanism* — that the thing
  came in with the clock and lives in the job rather than the object — but the tape runs out
  mid-sentence on the name. The mechanism is the players' to be told. The name is theirs to
  find.
- **The true ending is no longer gated on collectibles alone.** Collectibles now buy the
  *manner* of the ending, not the ending itself.
- **The between-nights beat is a police debrief**, not a shop. Wages and upgrades move to a
  phone menu.
- **Night 3's queue payoff gains a second meaning.** "Tell them to stop opening" is a man
  handing evidence to an investigation, which is what Walt was doing all along and what
  everyone — including the police — has been misreading for twenty-eight years.

---

## 9. Replay

The culprit never changes. A whodunnit with a rotating answer is a slot machine, and the
story only works if Wren is true.

What rotates on repeat playthroughs:
- **Clue locations**, from a pool of three per clue.
- **Which red herring gets extra support** that run — one wrong suspect gains two additional
  circumstantial clues, so different crews go down different wrong roads and disagree with
  each other afterwards.
- **Calder's opening theory**, which is Walt by default but can be Halvard or Kask.

**Cold Case mode**, post-launch: the same mall, a different missing-persons file, a
different answer, a different clue set. That is where a rotating culprit belongs.

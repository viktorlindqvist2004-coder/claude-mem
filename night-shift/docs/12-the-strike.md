# The Strike

> The set-piece the whole game is built toward. When the Great Clock finally does
> something, it has to be worth twenty-eight years of standing still.

---

## 1. The clock is digital

The face everyone sees is a **1986 seven-segment LED display**: four red digits and a
colon, about 2.4 m wide, mounted in a dark panel on the front of the clock housing. It
reads **03:33** and has done since the night of 14 November 1998.

Behind that panel is a **1904 turret movement from Northmoor Station**.

This is not a contradiction, it is the point. Eric Halvard bought the station movement at
auction in 1986 and did not restore it — it was 1986, he wanted his mall to look modern,
so he **cased the movement in and bolted a digital display to the front of it**. The
dedication plate that names Elias Wren is *behind that panel*, which is why Malik's tape
says he could not read it, and why prying the panel off on Night 5 is an act rather than
a glance.

A friendly modern surface bolted over something old that nobody ever dealt with. The mall
did to the clock exactly what the Night Manager does to himself.

**Design consequences**
- Red numerals are the only large light source in the atrium. Everything reads against them.
- A digital display can do things a dial cannot: flicker, blank, show all segments at once,
  count, show a number that is not a time.
- **`88:88`** — every segment lit — is the single most unsettling thing a seven-segment
  display can do, and it costs nothing. Spend it exactly twice in the whole game.
- Every other display in the building is wired to the same master clock: the tills, the
  time clock, the vending machines, the camera room timestamps, the microwave in the staff
  room. They can all be made to agree at once, which is worse than any of them alone.

## 3. The Strike

**Trigger.** Night 5, the moment the crew completes the third item of the closing routine —
whichever one they do last. The Night Manager has been talking throughout. He stops
mid-sentence.

Two seconds of nothing. Then the display goes **`88:88`**, and the first strike lands.

### The sound

Not a chime. A **station bell** — a single struck bronze note with a very long decay,
recorded close, played at a volume that is physically uncomfortable, with a sub-bass hit
under it that arrives 40 ms early so the room shakes before the ear registers the bell.

Between strikes there is no ambience at all. The hum of every fitting in the building has
stopped. That silence is what the crew will remember.

### It strikes thirty-three times

The crew will count. They will count out loud, over voice chat, because counting is what
people do when something is repeating and they want it to stop. They will get to twelve and
expect it to end, and it will not.

**This is the mechanic.** The count is the timer. It tells the crew exactly how much is
left, it is diegetic, and it means the tension curve is something the players are saying to
each other rather than a bar on screen.

One strike every four seconds. Two minutes and twelve seconds, start to finish.

### Phase one — strikes 1 to 11 · "wrong, but survivable"

- The display cycles: `88:88` on each strike, `03:33` between.
- The red emergency circuit **pulses in time with the bell** — full on the strike, nearly
  out between. The crew's shadows jump four metres with each one.
- Dust comes down from the ceiling void continuously for the rest of the sequence. In
  torchlight it is the most beautiful thing in the game.
- Every other display in the building — the tills, the time clock, the vending machines,
  the camera monitors — flips to `03:33` and begins flickering in unison.
- Loose objects on the shop shelves start to walk. Not fall: walk, a few millimetres per
  strike, all in the same direction, toward the atrium.

### Phase two — strikes 12 to 22 · "the building moves"

- **Strike 12** is where it should stop, and doesn't. Make the pause before 13 a beat longer
  than the others.
- Ceiling tiles drop in sequence down a wing, one per strike, running toward whoever is
  furthest from the atrium.
- The escalators start. All of them, all directions, some against each other.
- The handrails on the balconies move on their own, running like belts.
- The dry fountain **runs**. Water comes up out of a basin that has been capped since 1991
  and spills across the terrazzo, and every reflection in the atrium doubles.
- The shutters roll. All of them, simultaneously, to exactly half height, and stop.
- Every mannequin in the building turns to face the atrium. The crew does not see this
  happen — they just find it has happened, which is the rule the whole game runs on.
- The glass roof flexes. Through it, the clouds are moving far too fast.

### Phase three — strikes 23 to 33 · "breakdown"

- The building shakes hard enough to blur the camera between strikes.
- The hum returns, but wrong: every fitting bending pitch upward together, a semitone per
  strike.
- The display stops showing times. It shows `19:98`, then `14:11`, then `03:33`, then the
  crew's shift number, then a count of how many people are in the building — and it is one
  higher than it should be.
- The four shades appear at the entrance, in their 1998 uniforms, and stand watching. They
  do not help during this. They are waiting to see whether the crew finishes.
- The Night Manager is audible under the bell, not on the PA, saying the same sentence over
  and over, too quiet to make out until strike 31: *"You don't have to do this. You'd have
  been fine here."*
- **Strike 33** lands, and everything — bell, shaking, water, pulse, hum — stops inside one
  frame.

### After

Total silence, and the display reads **`06:00`**.

Then the fluorescents go out in banks, back of house first, and the sunrise comes through
the filthy glass.

## 4. What the crew is doing during it

The Strike is not a cutscene. Taking control away for two minutes after five nights of
earning it is the single worst thing this game could do.

**The task:** the display is at `88:88` and the closing routine has one step left that only
works while the clock is striking — the crew has to hold the **master timer panel** in the
plant room through all thirty-three, and the panel resets if it is left unattended for more
than eight seconds.

The plant room is in the basement. The atrium is where the shades are. The route between
them takes about forty seconds, and the panel needs someone on it continuously.

So: **a relay.** Two players minimum on the panel rotation, the others holding the route
open through a building that is actively rearranging itself. Solo players get a wedge that
holds the panel for 25 seconds at a time and have to sprint the loop nine times.

This is the only sequence in the game where the crew cannot all be in the same place, and
it is the last thing they do together.

---

## 5. Why thirty-three

Nobody explains it. The crew will work it out or they won't:

The last train out of Northmoor Station left at **03:33**. Elias Wren struck the bell once
for every minute past the half hour, every night, for forty-one years, so that the people on
the platform would know how long they had.

Thirty-three strikes is him telling them how long they have.

## 6. Build notes for Studio

- **Shake** is camera-only plus a handful of scripted prop nudges. Do not physics-enable the
  building; a mall of unanchored parts at 33 impulses is a crash, not a scare.
- **The bell** must be one sound played from the clock's position with a large
  `RollOffMaxDistance`, not a 2D track, so it arrives at different times in different parts
  of the mall. Someone in the basement hears it late and muffled, and that is correct.
- **The pulse** on the red circuit is one `SoundGroup` and one light-intensity tween driven
  by the same clock, so audio and light never drift apart.
- **Displays**: one shared `StringValue` drives every seven-segment display in the game.
  Changing it in one place changes forty objects, which is how `03:33` propagates.
- **Ceiling tile drops, shutter rolls and escalator starts** are pre-placed actors triggered
  by strike index, not spawned at runtime.
- **Budget the dust.** One emitter per occupied room, not per fitting.
